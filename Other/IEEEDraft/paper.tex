% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Automated freshness classification of fruits and vegetables is a
critical task for food safety, supply chain management, and retail
quality control.
This paper presents a systematic study of transfer learning applied to
this problem, evaluating four convolutional neural network backbone
architectures --- ResNet-18, MobileNetV3-Small, EfficientNet-B0, and
EfficientNet-B2 --- under four training conditions that vary the degree
of backbone fine-tuning and the presence of a non-linear projection head.
Experiments are conducted on six publicly available datasets comprising
over 55,000 images across multiple fruit and vegetable categories,
including binary (fresh/rotten) and three-class (fresh/formalin/rotten)
annotation schemes.
On the two datasets for which published baselines exist, our models
exceed the state of the art: on the apple/banana/orange benchmark our
best single model reaches a perfect 100\% accuracy and a 16-model
ensemble attains 99.96\%, surpassing the previously reported 99.61\%;
on the peach/pomegranate/strawberry benchmark our ensemble reaches
96.68\%, surpassing the published 95.0\%.
A key finding is that the optimal training condition differs by backbone
family: fine-tuning the last residual block benefits ResNet-18 (C4),
whereas MobileNetV3 and both EfficientNet variants perform best with a
completely frozen backbone and projection head (C3).
We further quantify domain shift on a held-out set of 219 real-world
mobile-camera photos of two fruits, showing that a model's per-fruit
accuracy is governed by whether that fruit appeared in training
(banana F1 of 71\% when seen versus 42\% when unseen), and we demonstrate
that a lightweight head-only fine-tuning step closes most of this gap,
raising macro F1 from 86.3\% to 94.5\%.
\end{abstract}

% ── I. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Food freshness is a critical quality attribute that directly affects
consumer health, retail profitability, and supply chain efficiency.
Manual inspection of fruits and vegetables is time-consuming, subjective,
and difficult to scale to the volumes required by modern distribution
systems.
Automated computer vision systems offer a scalable and objective
alternative, capable of processing large volumes of produce at low cost.

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) pre-trained on large-scale image datasets, have
demonstrated strong performance on a wide range of visual classification
tasks.
Transfer learning~\cite{pan2010transfer} leverages these pre-trained
representations to build effective classifiers from limited
domain-specific data --- an important property for food quality
applications where labeled datasets are expensive to collect.

Despite the growing body of work on food classification and freshness
detection, a systematic comparison of backbone architectures and
fine-tuning strategies under controlled conditions remains lacking.
Furthermore, the domain shift between publicly available dataset images
(typically acquired in controlled photographic conditions) and real-world
images captured by mobile devices has received limited attention.

This paper addresses both gaps through the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A controlled comparison of four CNN backbone architectures
    (ResNet-18, MobileNetV3-Small, EfficientNet-B0, EfficientNet-B2) and
    four training conditions (C1--C4) across six benchmark datasets
    totalling 55,040 images, in both binary and six-class
    (fruit$\times$state) formulations.
    \item State-of-the-art results on the two datasets with published
    baselines: a perfect 100\% accuracy (best single model) and a 99.96\%
    16-model ensemble on the apple/banana/orange benchmark, and a 96.68\%
    ensemble on the peach/pomegranate/strawberry benchmark, both exceeding
    previously reported figures.
    \item The identification of a consistent interaction between backbone
    family and optimal training condition: C4 (partial fine-tuning +
    projection head) for ResNet-18 vs.\ C3 (frozen backbone + projection
    head) for the MobileNet and EfficientNet families.
    \item A per-fruit domain-shift analysis on 219 real mobile-camera
    photos showing that generalization tracks training exposure, together
    with a head-only fine-tuning recipe that raises in-domain macro F1
    from 86.3\% to 94.5\%.
    \item An open-source pipeline implementing the full experimental
    workflow from data preparation to ensemble construction and
    benchmark reporting.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the methodology.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents experimental results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses key findings.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

% ── II. Related Work ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Food Quality Classification with Deep Learning}

Deep learning has been widely applied to food recognition and quality
assessment~\cite{automatic2023classification}.
Several works specifically target freshness detection using CNNs trained
from scratch or with transfer
learning~\cite{fresh2023rotten,iqbal2025canned}.
Bangladeshi et al.\ evaluated multiple deep learning architectures for
fresh/rotten detection of fruits and vegetables, finding that pre-trained
models significantly outperform models trained from
scratch~\cite{bangladeshi2022fresh}.

\subsection{Backbone Architectures}

ResNet~\cite{he2016resnet} introduced skip connections to address the
vanishing gradient problem, enabling deeper networks that generalize well
across domains.
EfficientNet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} proposed compound scaling ---
simultaneously adjusting network depth, width, and input resolution ---
achieving superior ImageNet accuracy with fewer parameters than
comparably-performing ResNet variants.
EfficientNet-B0 and B2 achieve 77.1\% and 80.1\% top-1 accuracy on
ImageNet respectively, compared to 69.8\% for ResNet-18, with
approximately half the parameters.

\subsection{Transfer Learning Strategies}

The most common transfer learning strategies for image classification
range from linear probing (training only the final classifier)
to full fine-tuning (updating all weights)~\cite{pan2010transfer}.
Kornblith et al.~\cite{kornblith2019better} showed that better ImageNet
features transfer better across tasks.
The intermediate strategy of fine-tuning only the later layers ---
which capture more task-specific features --- is widely adopted as a
balance between adaptation and catastrophic
forgetting~\cite{kirkpatrick2017catastrophic}.
Combining multiple models into an ensemble by averaging their predicted
class probabilities is a long-established technique for reducing variance
and exploiting complementary errors across
classifiers~\cite{dietterich2000ensemble}; its benefit is largest when the
constituent models are diverse and the task is not already saturated.

\subsection{Prior Results on the Benchmark Datasets}

Two of the datasets used here have published baselines that enable a
direct comparison.
On the Kaggle apple/banana/orange collection,
Palakodati et al.~\cite{palakodati2020fresh} trained a custom CNN and
reported 97.82\% accuracy, while
Chakraborty et al.~\cite{chakraborty2021implementation} obtained 99.61\%
with a MobileNetV2 transfer-learning model.
On the Mendeley peach/pomegranate/strawberry collection,
Sharma and Kumar~\cite{sharma2025automated} fine-tuned ResNet50 and
reported 95.0\% accuracy.
All three report accuracy as their primary metric, which we adopt for the
comparison in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Datasets}

Six publicly available datasets are used in this study
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).
Together they cover 55,040 images across fruits, vegetables, and citrus
varieties, with binary freshness labels (fresh/rotten) in five datasets
and a three-class annotation (fresh/formalin/rotten) in one.
The FruitVision dataset is particularly relevant as the formalin class
presents a significant challenge: chemically-treated samples are
visually similar to fresh specimens, analogous to the difficulty of
detecting subtle spoilage in fermented foods.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

All images are resized so the shortest side is 256 pixels and then
center-cropped to $224 \times 224$ for validation.
Training images undergo the following augmentation pipeline:
random resized crop to $224 \times 224$,
random horizontal flip,
$\pm 30^{\circ}$ rotation,
color jitter (brightness, contrast, saturation, hue),
and Gaussian blur (kernel size 3).
All images are normalized with ImageNet channel statistics
($\mu = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406]$, $\sigma = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]$).
A stratified 80/20 train/validation split with fixed random seed 42
is applied to all datasets.

\subsection{Backbone Architectures}

Four pre-trained CNN backbones are evaluated, all initialized with
ImageNet weights from the \texttt{torchvision} library:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ResNet-18}~\cite{he2016resnet}: 11.2M parameters,
    512-dimensional feature output from global average pooling.
    \item \textbf{MobileNetV3-Small}: 2.5M parameters,
    576-dimensional feature output; the most compact backbone, designed
    for mobile and embedded inference.
    \item \textbf{EfficientNet-B0}~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}: 5.3M
    parameters, 1280-dimensional feature output.
    \item \textbf{EfficientNet-B2}~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}: 9.1M
    parameters, 1408-dimensional feature output.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Conditions}

Four training conditions form an ablation study over two independent
dimensions: (1) whether the backbone is partially fine-tuned, and
(2) whether a non-linear projection head is used before the classifier.
Table~\ref{tab:conditions} summarizes all four combinations.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Training conditions — ablation over fine-tuning and head architecture.}
\label{tab:conditions}
\begin{tabular}{lp{1.8cm}p{1.8cm}p{2.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{ID} & \textbf{Backbone} & \textbf{Head} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
C1 & Frozen      & Linear only         & Linear probe \\
C2 & Layer4 free & Linear only         & Partial fine-tuning \\
C3 & Frozen      & Proj.\ + Linear     & Full head, frozen backbone \\
C4 & Layer4 free & Proj.\ + Linear     & Full head + fine-tuning \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{C1 — Linear probe (frozen backbone, linear head).}
All backbone weights are locked throughout training.
Only a single linear layer $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{out} \times C}$
is optimized, where $d_\text{out}$ is the backbone output dimension and
$C$ the number of classes.
This is the classical \textit{linear probe} setting~\cite{kornblith2019better},
which evaluates the quality of pre-trained features without any domain
adaptation.
It is the fastest and most data-efficient condition, but cannot correct
for any mismatch between ImageNet features and the target domain.

\textbf{C2 — Partial fine-tuning (layer4 free, linear head).}
The last residual block of the backbone (\texttt{layer4}, comprising
approximately 2M parameters for ResNet-18 and proportionally more for
EfficientNet) is unfrozen and trained jointly with the linear classifier.
All earlier layers remain frozen.
This allows the highest-level feature detectors to adapt toward
freshness-specific representations while retaining the general low-level
and mid-level features learned from ImageNet.
The linear head is the same as C1, providing no additional non-linear
capacity beyond the unfrozen backbone block.

\textbf{C3 — Projection head with frozen backbone.}
The backbone is fully frozen (as in C1), but the simple linear classifier
is replaced by a two-layer non-linear projection head followed by a
linear output layer:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h} = \text{ReLU}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_2\,
        \text{ReLU}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_1\,\mathbf{f}\right)\right),
    \quad \hat{y} = \mathbf{W}_3\,\mathbf{h}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{out}}$ is the backbone feature
vector, $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times d_\text{out}}$,
$\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 256}$, and
$\mathbf{W}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times 128}$.
The non-linear projection can reshape the feature space to form more
discriminative decision boundaries without requiring backbone fine-tuning.
This condition is particularly relevant when the pre-trained features
are already highly expressive, as is the case for larger EfficientNet
variants.

\textbf{C4 — Full head with partial fine-tuning.}
This condition combines the projection head of C3 with the backbone
fine-tuning of C2.
The last backbone block (\texttt{layer4}) and the full projection head
are optimized jointly, using a dual learning rate strategy:
$\text{lr} = 10^{-3}$ for the projection head and classifier, and
$\text{lr}_\text{backbone} = 10^{-5}$ for the backbone block.
The reduced backbone learning rate is essential to prevent catastrophic
forgetting~\cite{kirkpatrick2017catastrophic}: aggressive updates to the
backbone destroy the general-purpose representations learned during
ImageNet pre-training, effectively resetting the transfer learning benefit.
By using a learning rate two orders of magnitude smaller, the backbone
adapts \textit{gradually} toward freshness-relevant features while
preserving the hierarchical feature structure inherited from pre-training.

All models are trained for 60 epochs using the Adam optimizer
($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$) with cosine annealing learning
rate schedule and weight decay $\lambda = 10^{-4}$.
Batch size is 32.

\subsection{Label Modes}

Each dataset is trained under two label formulations.
In \textbf{state} mode the classes are the freshness states only
(fresh/rotten, or fresh/formalin/rotten), so the classifier is
fruit-agnostic.
In \textbf{fruit\_state} mode each fruit$\times$state combination is a
distinct class (e.g.\ \texttt{apple\_fresh}, \texttt{apple\_rotten}),
yielding six classes for the two three-fruit benchmark datasets.
The fruit\_state formulation matches the protocol of the published
baselines and is therefore used for the head-to-head comparison
(Section~\ref{sec:bench}).

\subsection{Model Ensemble}
\label{sec:ensmethod}

For the two benchmark datasets, the sixteen fruit\_state models
(four backbones $\times$ four conditions) are combined into a single
classifier by averaging their predicted class-probability vectors.
Let $\mathbf{p}^{(m)}_i \in \Delta^{C-1}$ be the softmax output of model
$m$ for image $i$.
The ensemble prediction is $\hat{y}_i = \arg\max_c \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m\,
p^{(m)}_{i,c}$, with three weighting schemes evaluated:
\textbf{mean} ($w_m = 1/M$),
\textbf{weighted} ($w_m \propto$ model $m$'s validation F1), and
\textbf{adaptive} ($w_m \propto$ model $m$'s confidence on image $i$).
The ensemble is computed entirely from the saved per-image probability
files, requiring no GPU or model reloading.

\subsection{Domain-Adaptation Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:ftmethod}

To adapt a model to a new visual domain with minimal data, the backbone
is kept frozen and only the classifier head is re-trained on a small set
of target-domain images.
This is evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation
(30 epochs, $\text{lr}_\text{head} = 5\times10^{-4}$, batch size 16),
reporting the mean macro F1 before and after adaptation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Given the potential class imbalance in food datasets, accuracy alone is
insufficient. Let $TP_c$, $FP_c$, $FN_c$ denote true positives, false
positives, and false negatives for class $c$, and $C$ the number of
classes.
We report:
\begin{align}
    \overline{F1} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}
    \frac{2\,TP_c}{2\,TP_c + FP_c + FN_c} \\
    \text{MCC} &= \frac{TP \cdot TN - FP \cdot FN}
    {\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}
\end{align}
Additionally, we report Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC) and
per-class F1, precision, and recall.
For food safety applications, recall on the \textit{rotten} class is
particularly critical: a false negative (rotten food classified as fresh)
poses a direct health risk.

Section~\ref{sec:ensmethod}.

On KFR, where the best single model is already perfect, the ensemble

model with weaker members only adds noise.

four distinct backbone families produce complementary errors when the task
is not already solved.

\subsection{Domain Shift and Per-Fruit Analysis}
\label{sec:domain}

To measure generalization to real-world conditions, the state-mode models
are evaluated on \textit{own\_dataset}: 219 mobile-camera photos of two
fruits --- 121 strawberry (64 fresh, 57 rotten) and 98 banana
(46 fresh, 52 rotten) --- used only for evaluation.
Across all 80 evaluated models the mean F1 drop from training to this set
is 21.8 points (range $-1.6$ to $51.5$).
Crucially, the drop is not uniform across fruits but is governed by
training exposure (Table~\ref{tab:perfruit}): models whose source dataset
\emph{included} banana average 71.4\% F1 on banana, whereas models that
\emph{never saw} banana collapse to 42.0\% --- barely above chance ---
while strawberry F1 is essentially unaffected.
The worst performers in Table~\ref{tab:domain} are precisely the
Lemon-Varieties (MLM) models, which never saw either evaluation fruit and
fall below 55\% despite $>$92\% training F1.

\paragraph{Domain-adaptation fine-tuning.}
The domain gap can be closed cheaply.
Starting from the EfficientNet-B0 head trained on Fruits Quality
(KFQ-EB0-C1) and fine-tuning only the head on \textit{own\_dataset} with
the backbone frozen (Section~\ref{sec:ftmethod}), macro F1 rises from
86.3\% to \textbf{94.5\%} ($+8.2$ points) under 5-fold cross-validation,
with the safety-critical rotten-class recall improving in step.
Because only the lightweight head is updated, this adaptation takes seconds
and needs no backbone storage --- a practical recipe for deployment:
pre-train on abundant public images, then fine-tune the head on a small
set of in-domain photos.

% ── V. Results and Discussion ─────────────────────────────────
\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Finding 1: C3 is optimal for MobileNet/EfficientNet; C4 for ResNet-18.}
Across both state and fruit\_state modes, MobileNetV3-Small and both
EfficientNet variants achieve their best performance under C3 (frozen
backbone + projection head), while ResNet-18 consistently benefits from
partial fine-tuning (C4).
We attribute this to the richer pre-trained features of the
MobileNet/EfficientNet families: their 576--1408-dimensional outputs
already capture discriminative cues for freshness without backbone
adaptation, whereas ResNet-18's 512-dimensional output benefits from
specialized high-level feature adaptation.
Fine-tuning these backbones with the available data sizes appears to
introduce overfitting rather than beneficial specialization.

\textbf{Finding 2: Dataset size determines the winning backbone.}
Below approximately 500 training samples, the compact backbones (ResNet-18
and MobileNetV3-Small, both 91.6\% on KFQ) outperform EfficientNet-B0
by up to 5.5 percentage points (86.1\%).
Above 1,500 training samples, EfficientNet-B0 consistently matches or
surpasses the other backbones, winning four of six datasets.
This is consistent with the larger head of EfficientNet requiring more
data to generalize.

\textbf{Finding 3: Our models exceed the published state of the art.}
On both datasets with published baselines, our results surpass prior work
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}).
On KFR our best single model (EfficientNet-B0/MobileNetV3 under C3) reaches
a perfect 100\% accuracy and the ensemble 99.96\%, above the 99.61\%
reported with MobileNetV2~\cite{chakraborty2021implementation}.
On MFR the ensemble reaches 96.68\%, above the 95.0\% reported with
ResNet50~\cite{sharma2025automated}.
The perfect KFR result (MCC $=$ 1.0, AUC-ROC $=$ 1.0) indicates that the
fresh/rotten distinction in this dataset is highly discriminative and fully
captured by frozen pre-trained features.

\textbf{Finding 4: Ensembling helps only where headroom remains.}
Combining the 16 fruit\_state models yields opposite outcomes on the two
datasets (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}).
On KFR, where the best single model is already perfect, the ensemble
marginally dips ($-0.03$ pts) because averaging a saturated model with
weaker members can only add noise.
On MFR, where the best single model reaches 95.72\%, the ensemble adds
$+0.85$ pts of F1.
The effect is invariant to the weighting scheme, confirming that the gain
stems from genuine error diversity across the four backbone families rather
than from any particular weighting heuristic.

\textbf{Finding 5: Formalin remains a fundamental challenge.}
FruitVision is the only dataset where no backbone exceeds 92.4\% F1-score,
despite having 10,154 images.
The formalin class is chemically treated to appear visually similar to
fresh samples, creating a subtle shift difficult to detect from RGB images
alone.
MobileNetV3-Small achieves the best result (92.4\%), narrowly ahead of
EfficientNet-B2 (92.3\%).
This is directly relevant to detecting subtle spoilage in fermented foods.

\textbf{Finding 6: Generalization tracks per-fruit training exposure.}
On the 219-photo real-world set, a model's accuracy on a given fruit is
governed by whether that fruit appeared in training
(Table~\ref{tab:perfruit}): banana F1 averages 71.4\% for models that saw
banana versus 42.0\% for those that did not, while strawberry F1 is
essentially unchanged ($\approx$80\% either way).
This explains the bottom of Table~\ref{tab:domain}: the worst models are
all Lemon-Varieties models, which never saw either evaluation fruit and so
fail catastrophically despite $>$92\% training F1.
High in-domain accuracy therefore does not imply good generalization to
unseen fruits or visual domains.

\textbf{Finding 7: Lightweight head fine-tuning closes the domain gap.}
Freezing the backbone and re-training only the classifier head on a small
set of in-domain photos raises macro F1 from 86.3\% to 94.5\%
($+8.2$ points) under 5-fold cross-validation.
Because only the head is updated, the adaptation is fast and storage-free,
offering a practical deployment recipe: pre-train on abundant public
images, then adapt the head to the target domain.

% ── VI. Conclusion ────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a systematic comparison of transfer learning
strategies for fruit and vegetable freshness classification.
Four backbone architectures (ResNet-18, MobileNetV3-Small,
EfficientNet-B0, EfficientNet-B2) and four training conditions were
evaluated across six benchmark datasets totalling 55,040 images, in both
binary and six-class formulations, and combined into per-dataset
ensembles.
Generalization was measured against 219 real-world photos of two fruits.

The key findings are:
(1) on the two datasets with published baselines, our models exceed the
state of the art --- a perfect 100\% accuracy (best single) and 99.96\%
(ensemble) on the apple/banana/orange benchmark, and 96.68\% (ensemble) on
the peach/pomegranate/strawberry benchmark;
(2) the optimal training condition differs by backbone family --- C4 for
ResNet-18, C3 (frozen backbone + projection head) for the MobileNet and
EfficientNet families --- indicating that richer pre-trained features
reduce the need for backbone fine-tuning;
(3) ensembling the four backbone families helps only where headroom
remains, adding $+0.85$ F1 points on the harder dataset but slightly
reducing the already-saturated one;
(4) real-world generalization is governed by per-fruit training exposure,
with banana F1 falling from 71\% to 42\% when the fruit is unseen;
(5) a lightweight, backbone-frozen head fine-tuning step raises in-domain
macro F1 from 86.3\% to 94.5\%, providing a practical domain-adaptation
recipe.

Future work will extend this pipeline to Korean food categories
(kimchi, rice cake, fermented side dishes), where the visual similarity
between fresh and fermented states is directly analogous to the
formalin challenge identified in this study, applying the same
public-pretrain then head-adapt recipe to bridge the domain gap.
